Good But Transient Games in Social Networks

12 February 2026

Every game theorist in office and armchair likes to discover and explore the exploitability of things. A game that works perfectly if played by only honest collaborators, but in which one dishonest cheater can gain more from the game, is an exploitable game waiting to be challenged, altered, and probably ruined. The cheater exploits the setup, takes more than gives, and the honest collaborators benefit less from their own constraint. More players then convert to become cheaters, because why play honestly to only see the dishonest collect big? Or as some say, blame the game, not the player.

The game theoretical view looks for the equilibrium of a game — solve for the equilibrium, the dictum goes. What is the stable arrangement of a game setup, because that’s where games will end up, barring intervention. Rational players follow the incentives and change the places they flock to.

But equilibria are not instantaneous, so dynamics can matter. And few games, other than those on boards and in virtual entertainment, are divorced from external conditions that too are in flux. If there are multiple dynamics relevant to a game, then intermediate and transient states can be found as the dynamics of the game progress on its paths to some equilibrium.

A concrete game is being played on LinkedIn. I was an early adopter and I have followed that platform for a long while. It has worsened. There can be many reasons for that, but I think that an interplay of game dynamics is a fruitful explanation of some undesirable changes on LinkedIn. An endogenous incentive shift based on exploitable features and their dynamics makes sense of observable changes.

The Benefits Of the Sudden LinkedIn-Level Market Scale

Look back at the world before LinkedIn or similar large-scale digital networks. Job-matching had then to be a more local affair, both geographically speaking, and much more built around who-knows-who in friendship networks. I recall some old suggestions that after an application was filed, it was prudent to call up the hiring manager and ask about progress, not so much because you really needed answers, but rather because the act signalled extra interest, and putting a voice to the words on the page might grant you an extra look. It was a smaller world.

A feature that followed from this condition was that a greater degree of honesty and commitment in the application was rational. This is not to say that inflated claims were absent, just that they were riskier to make, more likely subject to scrutiny, and so, the rational man acted accordingly. A culture evolved alongside this, encoding what degree of salesmanship, omission or rebranding of one’s past was acceptable. And the culture works its ways often invisibly and subconsciously.

Then when LinkedIn emerged suddenly in that cultural environment, it was a boon. The size of the job market grew massively. An application that may have been limited in scope by a person’s ability to find corporate websites and addresses, or by geography and friendship network, suddenly could, with modest effort, reach far more potential employers, well beyond the old borders. This was a win-win arrangement. It is in the interest of both the job-seeker and the company that the best match takes place. And more productive companies can serve customers better, be more innovative, produce additional spill-over, and generally elevate the societies they are part of.

I have both been hired, and hired others through LinkedIn in the early days. Especially when dealing with specialized technical expertise, well-connected global job markets are very helpful, even more so for companies and persons who do their business and live their lives outside the major hubs. If the only specialized people one can realistically reach and hire are those graduating at nearby universities, then being close to said places is key. If, however, competent people and awesome companies are findable even far away from the name-brand educational institutions, fewer constraints are imposed on all. This is good in many ways.

But Culture Changes and Friction Grows

So something as helpful as LinkedIn became broadly adopted and application volume grew. As noted, this is part of the upside of the larger markets that opened more opportunity. However, it also meant that any single claim about one’s accomplishments became less likely to matter and less likely to be scrutinized. The time spent on the vast majority of applications is measured in a few seconds. So if the application at the top of the screening funnel serves one, and only one goal of the applicant – to lead to the next step in the multi-step hiring process – then whatever it takes to solve that problem is rational.

The old culture of what was the risk-appropriate level of presentation began to give way to a culture of bold claims, optimized keywords, and just enough to pass any initial filter. The completely fraudulent claims are still discovered at later stages. But there is a lot of space between that extreme and the unvarnished, truthful disclosure. The rational strategy shifted as the manner applications were evaluated changed alongside the size and scale of the market.

The reaction to that is that the value of independent and readily recognizable certification grows. I recall LinkedIn exploring recommendations and endorsed skills, even providing small skills tests one could take. There are even ways nowadays to certify, via passport and video, that one is an actual human—a low bar for being considered an applicant worth further consideration. External sources of skill validation have become valuable too, where in data science some years ago, a few Kaggle projects and scores could count for a lot, which required actual work and competence to complete. More recently, the popular language learning app Duolingo can be used to convey quantifiable language skills on the personal LinkedIn page.

But perhaps the easiest external validation to filter on is educational institutions and prior employers. For example, my friend struggled to gain any traction in job searches. Until he was finally hired by a large name-brand company. Then recruiters flocked to him. His competence had not changed, his signals had, and they were the rate-limiting factor to become part of a larger, more liquid job-matching market.

The final and thickest external validation is that someone the employer knows and trusts enough to have hired, points to a person and says, ‘that one is good.’ For some job applications nowadays, there are special fields to note who internally referred you there. And so even the online world of vast markets is back to what that old-school guy with his desk telephone and aggressively firm handshakes thought was the only proper way to do business, and that was with people who had been recommended to you.

Echoes of the Old Ways Are Heard

I think the LinkedIn boon was inherently unstable. The norms that made it work at its scale, were not individually rational. This is at the heart of many game theoretical dilemmas, say the tragedy of the commons. However, culture and the blurry norms do not change as fast as digital technology, and therefore the world the early LinkedIn entered benefitted all players immensely and for several years.

Nowadays it is more common to note that this lag in cultural adaptation is a problem. Say for example cultural, even biological, barriers to bullying or exploitation of teenagers fail to scale to the digital technological condition. I think that can be true in certain cases, where the cultural lag exposes some online to unique suffering. However, in the case of LinkedIn, the lagging norms were central to the broad benefits of early LinkedIn. It was however an exploitable game, and despite attempts by its management and product teams to correct some trends, the professional network site degraded.

I do not wish to hypothesize on the exact degree of the changes I sketched above. For example, early LinkedIn by no means completely ended the returns on real-world connections and old-fashioned trust networks. The impersonal, meritocratic order, even where its ethos is the strongest, is never a complete story where humans are concerned. Nor have the current stage of a degraded LinkedIn brought us back to the mostly local job markets of the pre-Internet days. For one, advertising to a global audience has never been cheaper, and organic reach to thousands of people is possible even for one person with a bit of wit, smarts, looks and hot takes, or some selection thereof.

But during the lifetime of LinkedIn, the personal network and social capital have returned to job matching efforts, albeit mutated from before, and also supercharged. An application in the form of an honest disclosure is not the way forward, which also means the call for such disclosures in the hope of finding the next hire is increasingly hopeless. Selection favours the socially skilled and tactical, who play the game of becoming findable well. That probably always was the case. But the online scale and transparency of early LinkedIn reduced that relative benefit. And that in itself was a good change.

Worse But Not By Design

Presently, many of the digital platforms of the Web 2.0 era are considered in part degraded and undesirable. I think a more nuanced critique is appropriate. Though something has been lost and some virtual places have taken a darker turn, I am much more doubtful that it all came about by design or plain greed.

Consider for example the concept of enshittification, by Cory Doctorow . It has become a popular term to describe and diagnose how many great Web 2.0 services lost their appeal. This term is sometimes naming a symptom: that social networks, or virtual spaces generally, degrade and become bloated with lots of other stuff than the original attraction. It sometimes also denotes a diagnosis of the symptom. Typically, the profit motive of the Big Tech companies is said to make them add more annoying ways to extract cash from their users and advertisers, while the users find themselves unable to exit the online services, since too much of life and commerce are mediated by said services.

I don’t doubt there are instances where that is a valid explanation of undesirable features. Recent disclosures that Meta knowingly earns decent cash on fraudulent ads creates perverse incentives, and fraudulent ads are unambiguously shitty. However, I think there are other dynamics at work too, which are not about squeezing max cash out of a captured user base. I outlined one above.

It is hardly in LinkedIn’s interest that their rapid growth and success eroded the norms beyond the digital service, which undergirded a key value of LinkedIn. At least some of LinkedIn’s product features, which arguably are part of the gumming up of the site, appear to me as attempts to fight against the effects of the norm erosion. We may be of the opinion that the attempts are futile or doing more harm than good. But even taking that critical stance, it is not enshittification. Rather, it is an exploitable game finding a new equilibrium, and teams motivated by profit, attempting to find ways to rebalance the game.

Neither is LinkedIn immune to exits. If in order for the personal profile on that service to be useful, a great deal of real-world backing-up and network by recognizable and respected sources are required, then at some point the reward of having a profile on the global platform shrinks. It may then have less to do with job-matching and just turn into another way to opine. The last time I was in charge of hiring in a startup, we had concluded there was too much noise in the LinkedIn applications, so we stuck to a few smaller sites specific to technical domains and nearby geography.

These are losses, and not for LinkedIn alone. As I argued above, early LinkedIn was a creator of opportunities. I do not like that social capital and tactical approaches to getting to know the “right people” matter as much as I think they do in many cases. I think the profit motive of LinkedIn makes them want to return to the early LinkedIn, more or less.

I am unsure though if they can because despite their commanding size, these platforms are not worlds upon themselves. They are integrated into a world with its cultures and games. If the greatness of the early LinkedIn indeed was a non-equilibrium phenomenon caused by culture and technology in flux, then there is no easy software reset available to solve the problem. That doesn’t mean we must be resigned to Kafkaesque hiring practices or all learn the lingo and manners of fitting in with networking. Creative new ways are possible. But the particular moment of early LinkedIn or any other massive-scale job-matching service may be gone, and any fix to be found outside these types of platforms.

Culture and Technology Dynamics

I have used LinkedIn to illustrate how simple game theoretical analysis may fail to make specific predictions at a set time, yet prove useful in pointing out a direction of change and not necessarily good change from rational, individual acts alone. Badness or dysfunction is usually emergent, not imposed, in the game theoretical view.

Theory is necessarily a simplification, which isolates some features of the world in a set of tractable equations. This can mean that some parameters of the world model are understood as constant, such as the culture the game is played within. This can in a local and limited sense be a good assumption. But if the game is influential enough, it can generate feedback into the culture and alter the condition of the game.

I argued this indeed took place on LinkedIn. An initial condition yielded great rewards when the vast LinkedIn network was launched and scaled rapidly, however, the change was great enough that it altered the conditions of the game substantially, and a decline followed. It was a hopeful beginning that did not last.

We can consider if other social networks or virtual designs are on similar trajectories. LinkedIn is fairly unique in its service, though, and therefore also its user base, so I caution against a too great generalization of what I have argued above. Still, technology can change fast and be widely adopted, so I expect interesting non-equilibrium states are the rule rather than the exception as culture and individuals discover new ways to engage with the game.
